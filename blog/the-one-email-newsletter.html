The One-Email Newsletter (Why I Stopped Overcomplicating Mine)

Direct Answer

My newsletter is one short monthly email built from exactly four parts: a story, a number, a thank you, and one link. That's it. No sections, no multiple CTAs, no design pass. For nonprofits especially, this format keeps donors connected between asks, and people give again when they feel part of something, not when they get hit with a wall of text.

I overcomplicated my newsletter for years, and that's exactly why I kept not sending it. Every time I sat down to write one, it grew. A section for updates, a section for upcoming events, a section for a spotlight, three calls to action competing for the same click, and a design pass that took longer than the actual writing. By the time I had something I thought was "ready," I'd usually talked myself out of sending it, because it needed one more edit, one more section, one more pass. Months would go by. The newsletter that was supposed to keep people close to my work became the thing I dreaded opening a draft of.

The fix wasn't a better template or a longer content calendar. It was cutting the format down until there was nothing left to overthink. Now it's one short monthly email: a story, a number, a thank you, and one link. That's the whole thing. I sit down, I write those four parts, and I send it. No section I'm not sure about, no CTA I'm debating between. It gets written because there's nothing left to complicate.

Why this matters, especially for nonprofits

This hack applies to anyone who sends a newsletter, but it matters most for nonprofits, and it's the reason I keep bringing it up with the mission-driven organizations I work with. Donor retention doesn't just live in the campaign emails, the year-end appeal, the Giving Tuesday push, the gala invitation. It lives in the gaps between those asks. If the only time a supporter hears from you is when you need something, you've trained them to treat every email from your organization as a request for money.

A short monthly update that isn't asking for anything does the opposite. It shows a donor what their support is actually doing, in a specific, human way, without a donation button attached to every paragraph. That's what keeps them emotionally connected to the mission between the moments you do need to ask. And when the ask does come, it lands differently. People give again when they feel part of something, not when they get hit with a wall of text asking them to open their wallet one more time.

The four-part format, in detail

This isn't a loose idea, it's a specific structure. Every send has exactly these four parts, in this order, and nothing else gets added.

One story

Not a general update. A real, specific moment: something that happened this month that you could describe to a friend over coffee. A person helped, a moment in a program, a decision your team made and why. Specificity is what makes a story land. "We continued our work in the community this month" isn't a story, it's a placeholder. A single real moment, described plainly, does more than a paragraph of summary ever will.

One number

A concrete stat or result that shows impact, tied to the story if you can manage it. Numbers ground a story in something measurable, and they give a reader a reason to believe the story matters beyond the anecdote. It doesn't need to be dramatic. It needs to be real and specific to what actually happened.

One thank you

Genuine, and specific where you can make it specific. Not a generic "thank you for your continued support," but something closer to naming what the support made possible this month. A thank you that references the actual story above it reads as sincere. A thank you bolted onto the bottom of an email as a formality reads as exactly that.

One link

A single clear next step, not five competing links to your homepage, your events page, your donate page, your social channels, and your blog all at once. One link, pointing to the most relevant action for that month's story. A reader with one option is far more likely to click it than a reader deciding between five.

I didn't need a better newsletter template. I needed permission to stop adding to it.

Why simplicity beats length for actually getting sent

The reason this format works isn't that it's clever. It's that it's finishable. A four-part email with a fixed structure takes an hour, not an afternoon. There's no design decision to agonize over, no second CTA to debate, no "should we add a section for this" conversation that stalls the whole send. When a newsletter is simple enough to finish in one sitting, it actually goes out. When it isn't, it sits in drafts until it's too stale to send, and then the next month's version starts to feel even more overdue, which makes it harder to start.

Length doesn't correlate with how much a reader takes away from it either. A long newsletter with five sections asks a reader to do the work of finding what matters to them. A four-part email does that work for them before they open it. They know exactly what they're getting: a story, a number, a thank you, one thing to do next. That predictability is part of why it gets read, and part of why it gets opened again the following month.

✉️
The test I use before hitting send

If I can't fill in all four parts honestly this month, story, number, thank you, link, I don't pad the email to make it look fuller. I write what's real and send it short. A short, honest newsletter beats a padded one every time.

Cadence: monthly, and why consistency matters more than frequency

I send this once a month. Not weekly, not "whenever there's news," monthly, on a rhythm people can come to expect. Consistency matters more than frequency here. A newsletter that reliably arrives once a month, every month, builds more trust over time than one that goes out weekly for six weeks and then disappears for the rest of the year. Readers, and donors especially, learn to trust a pattern. An inconsistent one erodes that trust even if any single email in the run was good.

Monthly also keeps the format honest. It's a short enough window that you almost always have one real story and one real number worth reporting, without having to manufacture content to fill a weekly slot. If you're sending more often than you have real material, you end up padding, and padding is exactly what breaks the four-part structure that makes this work in the first place.

One hack, out of thirty

This is hack 19 in a series I never expected to keep going past thirty, because there's always one more piece of the day, or the business, quietly worth simplifying. If your newsletter has been sitting half-written for months the way mine used to, the fix probably isn't a better template. It's fewer parts. A story, a number, a thank you, one link, sent every month, whether or not it feels finished enough. Finished enough to send is the whole goal.
