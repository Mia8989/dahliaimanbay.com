Know Your One Number

Direct Answer

Instead of tracking every metric in my business, I pick one number that matters most this quarter, the metric most tied to whatever is actually constraining growth right now: new customers, monthly donors, retention, response time. I put it somewhere I see it daily, and I let it guide the choices I make until the quarter ends. Chasing every number moves none of them. Choosing one moves that one.

I used to track everything in my business, which is a fancy way of saying I improved nothing. I had dashboards for traffic, dashboards for engagement, dashboards for pipeline, dashboards for retention, dashboards for a dozen numbers I could recite but couldn't tell you what to do about. Every one of them was updating in real time. None of them was changing my behavior.

Why a full dashboard doesn't drive action

A dashboard full of numbers feels like control. You open it, you see everything, you feel informed. But being informed and being in control of the outcome are different things. When ten metrics are all competing for your attention, none of them wins. You end up making decisions the way you always did, out of habit or urgency, and just narrating the results afterward with whichever number happens to have moved.

That was the trap I was in. I wasn't short on data. I was short on a filter for what to do with it. Watching a number is not the same as being guided by it, and I was watching plenty.

How I actually choose the one number

The number isn't the metric that's easiest to measure or the one that looks best on a slide. It's the metric most tied to whatever is currently the biggest constraint on the business. That takes an honest answer to one question first: what is actually stopping growth right now, not what would be nice to improve.

If the honest answer is that I don't have enough customers coming in, the number is new customers. If the honest answer is that people are leaving faster than I can replace them, the number is retention, not new customers, because bringing more people through a leaking system doesn't fix the leak. If I run something donor-funded, and renewals are soft, the number is monthly donors. If clients are frustrated because they wait too long to hear back, the number is response time. The metric changes every quarter because the constraint changes every quarter. The method doesn't.

Putting it where you'll actually see it

Choosing the number is only half of it. The other half is making sure it's somewhere you see it every day without having to go looking for it, because a number buried in a report you open once a month isn't guiding anything, it's just archived. A whiteboard by your desk works. A recurring note pinned to the top of your notes app works. A simple dashboard you already glance at each morning works. The tool doesn't matter. What matters is that the number is in your line of sight before you start making decisions, not after.

Chasing every number moves none of them. Choosing one moves that one.

What changes when one number is steering

The real shift isn't in what you measure, it's in what you decide. Once a single number is sitting in front of you every day, it starts filtering choices you'd otherwise make on autopilot. Say retention is the number for the quarter. A request comes in to spend the week building a flashy new feature that would look great in a pitch, or a new lead magnet to pull in fresh signups. Both are reasonable asks on a normal week. But if retention is the number, the actual question becomes: does this move retention, or does it just feel productive? If it doesn't move retention, it waits. That's the whole mechanism. The number doesn't make the decision for you, it gives you a fast, honest test to run every decision through.

Without a chosen number, that same week gets filled with whatever feels most urgent in the moment, which is usually whatever showed up last in your inbox. With a chosen number, urgency stops being the tiebreaker. Relevance to the number is.

What happens at the end of the quarter

When the quarter ends, I look at what the number actually did. If it moved, that's worth stopping to notice, not just logging and moving on. If it didn't move, or moved the wrong direction, that's not a failure to bury, it's diagnostic information: either the number wasn't the real constraint, or the actions taken during the quarter weren't actually aimed at it. Both are useful to know. Then I ask the same question again: what's the biggest constraint now? Sometimes the answer is the same number for another quarter, because the work isn't done. Usually it's a different one, because fixing one constraint tends to expose the next.

You're not ignoring the other numbers

The pushback I hear most is some version of "but what about everything else?" I still track everything else. Retention, new customers, response time, whatever else matters to the business, all of it is still recorded, still visible if I want to check it. Choosing one number to focus on for a quarter isn't the same as ignoring the rest. It's deciding which one gets to steer, this quarter, and letting the others sit in the passenger seat instead of all shouting directions at once. Next quarter, one of them gets the wheel.

Key takeaways

Tracking everything improves nothing. A dashboard full of numbers creates the feeling of control without actually driving a decision.

✓

One hack, out of thirty

This is hack 26 in a series I never expected to keep going past thirty, because there's always one more piece of the day quietly worth simplifying. If tracking everything has ever left you feeling informed but stuck, start smaller than you think you need to: name the one constraint that's actually in your way this quarter, and let one number tell you whether you're closing in on it.
