Ask for the Referral (Even When It Feels Awkward)

Direct Answer

Referrals do not happen because people forget you exist. They happen because nobody asked directly. Go to your five happiest customers or donors and ask one specific question: "Do you know one person like you who'd benefit?" It is specific, it is easy to answer, and it turns word of mouth from something you quietly hope for into something you actually build.

Every video in my "30 Hacks From Dahlia" series ends with the same line, and this one is the reason I keep saying it: I help small teams do big things, and most of what that actually looks like is one short, slightly uncomfortable conversation that nobody wants to start first. This hack is that conversation.

Some of my best clients came from a referral I almost talked myself out of asking for. I remember sitting with the message half-written, telling myself it was too direct, that if the client wanted to send someone my way, they already would have. I sent it anyway, feeling a little exposed the whole time. That client became one of the strongest working relationships I have had, and the only reason it exists is because I asked instead of waiting to be offered.

Why this matters more than another marketing channel

Word of mouth is the cheapest growth channel available to any small business or nonprofit. There is no ad spend, no algorithm to guess at, and someone who already trusts you doing the convincing on your behalf. The problem is not that referrals don't work. The problem is that most of us treat them as something that should happen on its own, a byproduct of doing good work, instead of something we actually go out and ask for.

Hoping is not a strategy. A happy client can think about referring you for weeks and never act on it, not because they don't want to, but because it never rises to the top of their to-do list until you give it a reason to. The nudge is the whole mechanism. Without it, goodwill just sits there, unused.

How to find your five

Before you ask anyone, be honest about who actually belongs on the list. It is tempting to go by gut feeling, whoever comes to mind first, but gut feeling is a weak filter. Look for real signals instead.

Recent positive feedback

A thank-you email, a glowing comment in a meeting, a strong review, a donor who told you directly that your program mattered to them. These are the people who have already, on their own, told you they are glad they worked with you or gave to you. That is a much stronger starting point than someone you simply assume is satisfied.

Repeat engagement

Someone who renewed, came back for a second project, gave again this year, or has shown up consistently over time is telling you something with their actions, not just their words. Repeat behavior is a stronger signal than a single nice comment, because it costs them something to keep choosing you.

Put those two signals together and your list of five writes itself. If someone shows both recent positive feedback and repeat engagement, they go to the top.

The exact script, and why it works

The question is: "Do you know one person like you who'd benefit?"

That single sentence does three things a vague ask never does. It is specific: you are not asking for a list, you are asking for one name. It is easy to answer: they don't have to think hard about your whole network, just one person similar to themselves. And it is low-pressure: it invites a thought, not a commitment, which makes it far more likely someone actually replies instead of letting the message sit.

Compare that to the version most of us default to: "Let me know if you know anyone who might be interested." It sounds polite, but it puts all the work on the other person. They have to define who "anyone" means, remember to think of you later, and then take the initiative to reach out. Almost nobody does that unprompted. A specific, direct question closes that gap.

💬
Why specific beats vague

A vague ask asks someone to do research on your behalf. A specific ask only asks them to answer a yes or no question about one person they already know. The narrower the ask, the higher the odds you actually get a name back.

When to ask

Timing matters as much as wording. Ask right after a positive moment, not weeks later when the goodwill has cooled. That could be right after a project wraps and the client tells you it went well, right after a donation lands and you've sent a genuine thank you, or right after someone tells you directly that something you did made a difference for them.

It is the same instinct behind automating a review request the day after a job is done, while the client is still thrilled. A referral ask works on the same logic. The feeling is the fuel. Ask while it is still there, not after it has faded into the background of someone's week.

Referrals don't happen because people forget you exist. They happen because nobody asked directly.

Make it easy for them to actually follow through

Getting a "yes, actually, I do know someone" is only half the job. Most referrals stall not because the person didn't want to help, but because making the actual introduction took more effort than they had time for. Remove that friction and you remove the biggest reason good intentions never turn into an introduction.

Offer to be introduced directly. Ask if you can reach out to the person yourself once you have a name and a quick heads-up from your happy client. Or give your client the language to use, one or two sentences they can copy into a text or email exactly as written, so they don't have to sit down and compose something from scratch. Either option turns a vague intention into a five-second action.

What to do with the fear of asking directly

I felt the awkwardness of this myself, sitting with that half-written message. The fear is usually some version of: what if they say no, what if it feels transactional, what if I've misjudged how they actually feel about working with me. Those thoughts are normal, and they are almost never accurate.

The people you should be asking are the ones who have already told you, in words or in actions, that they value what you do. Asking them for a referral is not an imposition. It is giving someone who already likes you a simple, welcome way to help. The worst outcome is a "not right now," which costs you nothing. The best outcome is a client I still work with today.

Key takeaways

Before you ask for the next referral

✓
Pick five people using real signals. Recent positive feedback plus repeat engagement, not just who comes to mind first.
✓
Use one specific question. "Do you know one person like you who'd benefit?" beats any vague, open-ended version.
✓
Ask right after a positive moment. The goodwill is strongest right then, not weeks later.
✓
Remove the friction. Offer to make the introduction yourself, or hand them ready-to-use language.
✓
Send the message even when it feels awkward. The worst answer is a no. The best answer might be one of your best clients.

Frequently Asked Questions

Ask one specific question instead of a vague open door. "Do you know one person like you who'd benefit?" is direct, but it's easy to answer because it names exactly who you're looking for and it doesn't ask for a list. A specific ask reads as confident, not pushy. A vague one, like "let me know if you know anyone," reads as an afterthought and almost never gets a response.

Ask right after a positive moment, not weeks later when the goodwill has faded. That could be right after a project wraps well, after a donation lands and you've thanked them, or after they've told you directly that something you did worked. The same timing logic that makes an automated review request effective right after a job is done applies here: ask when the good feeling is fresh, not when you happen to remember.

Look for real signals, not just a gut feeling about who likes you. Recent positive feedback, a thank-you note, a strong review, a comment in a meeting, is a signal. Repeat engagement, someone who renewed, gave again, or has stuck with you over time, is a signal. Start with the people who show both, since they are the ones most likely to actually follow through when you ask.

Offering to handle the introduction removes the biggest reason people don't follow through: they mean to make the connection, then forget. Ask if you can reach out directly once they give you a name, or hand them one or two sentences they can forward as is. Either way, you are removing the work of writing something from scratch, which is usually what stalls a referral that someone genuinely wanted to make.
