Your Bio Link Is a Front Door, Not a Dead End

Direct Answer

You worked hard for that click. Don't waste it.

My link in bio used to be a dead end. One link, one page, and everyone who clicked just bounced. I'd spend real effort earning that tap, a good caption, a useful video, a comment that made someone curious enough to check my profile, and then I handed them a single destination that probably wasn't even the thing they wanted. Most of them left without doing anything at all.

So I turned it into a front door. A simple landing page with my top five actions: book a call, read the latest, shop, donate, subscribe. One tap, everything they might want. That's it. That's the whole hack. But the reason it works is worth slowing down on, because most people who fix their bio link stop at "add more buttons" and miss the part that actually matters.

You worked hard for that click. Don't waste it.

Every click on your bio link is a small, expensive thing. Someone stopped scrolling, read your post or watched your video, decided you were worth a few more seconds of their attention, and tapped through. That's the hardest part of the whole funnel. The algorithm doesn't hand you that moment twice. If the page on the other side of the tap doesn't give them a fast, obvious way to do the thing they actually came to do, you've spent that attention and gotten nothing back for it.

A single link, whatever it points to, assumes every visitor wants the same thing. They don't. Someone who just watched a case study video wants something different than someone who found you through a random comment thread. A front door page doesn't guess. It lays out the real options and lets each visitor pick the one that matches why they clicked.

How to pick your own top five actions

This is the part people get wrong first. It's tempting to fill the five slots with whatever you personally want people to do, mostly "buy from me" repeated five different ways. That's not what makes a front door work. The five actions should map to what different segments of your audience actually want, not just your own priorities.

Before you build anything, think through who actually lands on that link and what they're each looking for. In my case, that's someone ready to hire me and book a call, someone who wants to see my latest thinking before they decide, someone browsing merchandise or products, someone who wants to support the work without buying anything, and someone who just wants to keep hearing from me without committing to any of the above. Five different intents, five different buttons. None of them compete with each other because none of them are trying to serve the same person twice.

💡
The test for each button

Before you add an action to your five, ask who specifically it's for. If you can't name a segment of your actual audience that wants exactly that thing, it doesn't earn a slot. Five buttons that each serve a real intent will always outperform five buttons that all try to serve you.

How to order them: highest intent first

Once you know your five actions, the order isn't arbitrary. Put your highest-intent action first, the one that matters most to your business and that the most ready visitors are looking for. For me, that's booking a call. The person who's already decided they want to talk to me shouldn't have to scroll past four other options to find the thing they came for.

Everything below that gets less urgent as you go. Reading the latest content is a lower-commitment ask than booking a call, but a higher one than just subscribing. Subscribing is the lowest bar of all, someone isn't ready to do anything else yet, but they don't want to lose touch either. That's fine. Give that person a home too. Just don't put it above the actions that actually move your business forward.

Building it: a page you control versus a link-in-bio tool

You've got two real paths here, and both work.

A simple static page you control

This is what I built. A single page on my own domain with five clearly labeled buttons, styled to match my actual brand instead of a generic template. It takes more setup upfront, you're writing the page yourself or having it built, but you own it completely. It lives at my domain, not a third party's, and I can track exactly what I want to track without a paywall in the way.

A link-in-bio tool

Tools built specifically for this exist for a reason: they get you live in minutes. Pick a template, add your five links, done. Most include built-in click analytics without any setup on your end. The tradeoff is you're working inside someone else's design system, and the free tiers usually carry their branding, not yours. If you're just getting started and want something up today, that tradeoff is often worth it. If your bio link is a real part of how people find you, owning the page eventually pays off.

Neither path is wrong. The mistake is having neither, and sending every click to a single dead-end link instead.

Proof this works: here's mine

I didn't build this as a theory. It's live right now, and I use it the same way I'm describing here: five actions, ordered by intent, matched to what different people actually come looking for. Here's mine, if you want to see the actual page instead of just reading about the idea. It's the same front door every visitor gets, whether they found me through a video, a post, or a comment I left somewhere.

Measuring whether it's working

A front door page isn't a set-it-and-forget-it fix. Once it's live, watch which buttons actually get clicked. That tells you something your own assumptions can't: which of your five actions people genuinely want, which is often different from the one you assumed they'd want. If your "book a call" button gets a fraction of the clicks your "read the latest" button gets, that's not a failure, it's information about where your audience actually is right now.

Revisit the order every so often instead of setting it once and walking away. If a lower-priority action is consistently outperforming the one you put at the top, move it up. Your audience will tell you what they want if you're watching the clicks instead of guessing from your desk.
