Turn Projects Into Systems

Direct Answer

Every time I finish a job, I ask what part of it could become a template, a checklist, or an automation for next time. We stay small by starting from zero on every project. The ones that grow turn each job into a reusable system, so the next one starts faster.

I used to start every project from scratch and then wonder why I was always tired. Every new client, deliverable and campaign began with a blank page. I treated that as a fresh start. It was really a sign I hadn't kept anything from the last time. I was solving the same problems over and over with different names attached, and calling it hard work.

The end-of-project question

When I finish a job, before I move on, I ask one question: what part of this could be a template, a checklist, or an automation for next time? Not "was this a success." Not "what would I do differently." What did I just do that I'll do again, in some form, for someone else?

Most projects have at least one honest answer. The intake questions I always ask a new client on the first call. The steps I follow every time I launch a page, in the same order, because I learned the hard way what happens when I skip one. A repetitive step with no judgment in it that a tool could just run.

How to spot what's actually worth systemizing

Not everything from a finished project deserves to become a system. Some parts were one-off, specific to that client or that moment, and a template for them is overhead. I use two signals to sort them.

Anything you did more than once

A step that showed up twice in the same project, or that I recognize from a past one, is the clearest signal. A step that happens once doesn't need a template. A step that happens every time wastes effort every time it's rebuilt.

Anything that required remembering a lot of small details

If a step depended on me remembering small things (the right order, the setting I always forget to change, the field I always double check), it will eventually get missed. A checklist exists to hold those details so I don't have to.

💡
The test I actually use

If I can picture doing this exact step again for a different client next month, I capture it. If I can't, I let it go.

Template, checklist, or automation: they're not the same thing

I used to lump all of this under "documentation," which was part of the problem. The three forms are different, and picking the right one is what gets the system used instead of left in a folder.

A template is a starting document. You duplicate it and fill it in instead of rebuilding the structure: a proposal outline, a project brief, an email sequence, a slide deck skeleton. It saves less typing than it does decisions about what goes where, which is usually the slowest part of starting anything.

A checklist is a sequence to follow. You work through it in order, so nothing gets skipped and nothing depends on your memory that day: launch steps, onboarding steps, a pre-send review. It earns its place the first time it catches something you'd have missed.

An automation is something that runs without you. It's a step that needs execution but not your judgment: a recurring report, a follow-up email, a file that renames and files itself. Automations save the most time of the three, but only for steps with zero decision-making. Automate a step that still needs your judgment and you'll spend more time fixing what it got wrong than you saved.

A template saves you from a blank page. A checklist saves you from your memory. An automation saves you from doing the step at all.

Capture it right after you finish, not later

Do it immediately after you close out the project, not next week when you have "more time." I've tried waiting. It doesn't work. The edge case that almost tripped you up, the order the steps had to happen in, the line a client always asks about: they're sharp for a day or two and then gone. A week later, rebuilding that context takes almost as long as the work did.

So right after I wrap a project, before I open the next one, I spend a few minutes writing down the answer to that question. Not a polished document. Just enough that I, or whoever touches this kind of work next, isn't starting from nothing. The system gets refined the second or third time it's used. The first version just has to exist.

How the thirty hacks connect

The template from writing the SOP the second time, the checklist behind the two-minute rule, the automation that reads your newsletters: each one started as a project that got finished once and then turned into something reusable. A template used ten times has saved you nine blank pages. A checklist that catches one mistake a month has protected a client relationship you never had to think about. None of it looks dramatic. The fortieth project just starts faster than the fourth did.

I help small teams do big things, and a lot of that is making sure the useful parts of yesterday's work don't have to be reinvented tomorrow. Finish your current project, then spend ten minutes on the end-of-project question.
