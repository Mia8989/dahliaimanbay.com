Raise Prices Without Apologizing

Direct Answer

Lead a price increase with the value and the reason, never an apology. State the effective date, what's included, and the new rate in plain language, and stop there. Apologizing signals you don't believe in the value either, and clients read that hesitation faster than they read the number. The right clients stay. The ones who leave were never your best-fit clients.

I undercharged for years, and it wasn't because my work was worth less. It was because I was scared. Scared of the email that says "actually, never mind." Scared of a client quietly finding someone cheaper. Scared, if I'm honest, of finding out my work wasn't worth what I wanted to charge for it. So I priced from that fear instead of from the value I was actually delivering, and I told myself it was being reasonable.

Why this matters more than it looks like it does

Underpricing out of fear doesn't just cost you money in the moment. It trains your clients. Every invoice you send at a scared number teaches the person on the other end what to expect from you going forward. Raise your rate later and it feels, to them, like a change in the deal, even though the deal was always undervalued from the start. The fear doesn't stay contained to the first invoice. It compounds, quietly, for as long as you let it run the pricing.

I think a lot of people who struggle with this assume the fix is finding the "right" number, some formula that removes the discomfort entirely. It doesn't work that way. The fix is separating the number from the apology. You can charge a fair, confident rate and still feel a flutter of nerves sending the message. That flutter is normal. What matters is whether the message itself sounds like you believe what you're charging, or like you're bracing for someone to talk you out of it.

The message structure that actually works

When I raise a rate now, the message follows the same shape every time, and it's shorter than people expect. Three parts, in this order, with nothing extra layered on top:

The three parts of a price increase message

✓
Effective date. Say exactly when the new rate takes effect. "Starting in [month]," not "sometime soon" or "at some point." A specific date signals this is a decision, not a suggestion up for debate.
✓
What's included. Restate the scope of what the client is getting. This is the part people skip, and it's the part that does the actual persuading. The number means nothing on its own; the number next to the value is what lands.
✓
The new rate. State it plainly. "Here's the new rate." No cushioning, no comparison to what you used to charge, no explanation of your personal costs or circumstances. The rate is a business fact, not a confession.

That's the whole message. Effective date, what's included, new rate. No apology, no over-explaining, no "I hope this is okay." Confident and clear, the same tone you'd use to tell a client about anything else in the relationship.

The number was never the problem. The apology attached to it was.

Why apologizing undermines the message

Here's the part that took me the longest to understand: an apology doesn't soften a price increase, it undercuts it. When you say "I'm so sorry to have to do this" or "I hate raising prices, but," you are telling the client, in your own words, that you don't fully believe the new number is fair. If you don't believe it, why would they? Clients aren't just reading the rate. They're reading your certainty about the rate, and hedging language is the clearest signal of uncertainty you can send.

Compare the two versions of the same message in your head. One says "starting in [month], here's what's included and here's the new rate." The other says "I'm really sorry, I know this is a big ask, but I have to raise my rate starting in [month], I hope you understand." Same information. Completely different read. The second version invites negotiation because it sounds like the sender expects to be talked out of it.

💡
The confidence check

Before you send a price increase message, read it back and ask: does this sound like I believe the work is worth this? If there's a "sorry," a "hopefully," or a justification of your personal costs anywhere in it, cut it. State the change once and let it stand on its own.

How much notice to give

Give clients enough runway to plan around the change, not so much that it turns into a months-long negotiation window. A clear lead time before the new rate takes effect respects the relationship without inviting a drawn-out back-and-forth. The exact length matters less than the consistency: pick a date, communicate it once, and hold it. Moving the date after you've already sent it undoes the confidence the rest of the message worked to build.

When a client pushes back, or leaves

This is going to happen, and it's supposed to. Some clients will accept the new rate without a word. Some will ask questions, which is fair, and you can answer them the same way you delivered the original message: plainly, without re-litigating whether the increase is justified. And some will leave. That's the part that used to wreck me, and it's the part I've come around on the most.

A client who leaves over a fair, clearly communicated price increase was almost never your best client. They were often the client getting the most out of the relationship for the least return, the one you quietly dreaded invoicing because you already knew the math didn't work in your favor. Losing that relationship isn't a failure of the price increase. It's the price increase doing exactly what it was supposed to do: correcting a mismatch that was already there before you sent the message, you just hadn't priced it out loud yet.

The emotional side, for the people who struggle with this

If you're the person who rereads a price increase email eleven times before sending it, or drafts it and closes the laptop instead, I want to name the fear directly instead of talking around it. It's usually not really about the number. It's the fear of rejection wearing a business outfit. Somewhere underneath the invoice is a worry that if you ask for what the work is worth, you'll find out it isn't worth that, and that feels safer to avoid than to test.

That fear is normal. I don't think it fully goes away, even now. What changes is that you stop letting it write the message for you. You feel the nerves, you send the plain version anyway, and you let the outcome be the outcome. The clients who value the work will stay. The ones who don't were telling you something true about the relationship, just not in the way you wanted to hear it. Do it anyway. That's the whole hack.

Key takeaways

  • Lead with the effective date, what's included, and the new rate, in that order, with nothing else added.
  • Never apologize for a price increase. Apologizing signals you don't believe in the value, and clients notice that hesitation before they notice the number.
  • Give clients clear, consistent notice before the new rate takes effect, and don't move the date once you've sent it.
  • Expect some clients to push back or leave. That's the increase working, not a sign you did it wrong.
  • The clients who leave over a fair increase were rarely your best-fit clients to begin with.
  • The fear is normal. Feel it, send the plain message anyway, and let the outcome speak for itself.
