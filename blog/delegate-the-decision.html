Delegate the Decision, Not Just the Task

Direct Answer

For a long time I handed people tasks and then made them ask me before every step. That isn't delegation, it's outsourcing the typing. Real delegation means giving someone the goal, the guardrails they should not cross without checking in, and the authority to decide everything else inside those guardrails on their own.

I was the bottleneck in my own business for a long time, and I didn't want to admit it. Every task I handed off still had a string attached to me. Someone would take the first step, then stop and wait to be told the second one. I told myself I was staying close to quality, staying available, staying involved. What I was actually doing was making sure nothing moved without me standing behind it.

Here's what I was doing wrong. I handed off tasks but made people ask me before every step. That's not delegating, that's assigning homework and grading it in real time. If every choice on a project still routes back to me, I haven't hired help, I've hired hands that wait for instructions. Hands that wait cost me the same attention as doing the work myself, just spread across more interruptions.

Hired hands versus help

There's a real difference between the two, and it shows up in a single moment: the moment something doesn't go exactly the way I would have pictured it. Hired hands come back and ask what to do next. Help makes a call, tells me what they decided and why, and keeps moving. For years I didn't understand that the gap between those two outcomes wasn't about the person's skill. It was about what I had actually given them to work with.

When I looked honestly at how I was delegating, I saw the pattern everywhere. I'd hand off a task as a list of steps instead of an outcome, so no wonder people came back with a question at every fork in the road. I had built a system where the only person allowed to think was me, then wondered why I couldn't step away from any of it.

The three things every delegated task actually needs

Once I saw the pattern, the fix wasn't complicated, but it did require me to give up something: control over every small decision along the way. I started breaking every task down into three parts before handing it to anyone. Goal. Guardrails. Authority. If I couldn't fill in all three clearly, I wasn't ready to delegate the task yet. I was just ready to dump it on someone.

Goal what done actually looks like
Guardrails what they should not cross without checking in
Authority what they get to decide on their own

Goal: what does done actually look like

The goal is what done actually looks like, not the list of steps to get there. "Post three times a week on Instagram" is a task. "Grow the kind of engaged following that could become future clients, and know within a month if a format isn't working" is a goal. A goal tells someone what they're actually trying to achieve, so they can adjust the how without needing my permission first. Describe only the steps, and I've made myself the only person on the team who understands the point of the work.

Guardrails: the boundaries, not a script

Guardrails are the boundaries someone should not cross without checking with me first, and nothing more: budget ceilings, brand voice, anything touching a client relationship or a legal commitment, anything genuinely hard to undo. They aren't a script for how to do the work, they're the edges of the field someone gets to run in. The mistake I used to make was treating every personal preference as a guardrail. A preference is something I'd like to see. A guardrail is something that actually needs my sign-off before it happens.

Authority: the part I used to skip

Authority is everything left once the guardrails are drawn, and it's the part I used to skip entirely. Define the goal and the guardrails and still expect every decision inside those lines to come back to me, and I haven't delegated anything, I've just added a step. Authority means someone can pick the wording, choose the send time, rewrite the sentence, push back on a vendor, without asking first. They report what they decided; they don't request permission to decide it.

How I actually say this out loud

The framework only works if I can actually say it out loud, so here's roughly how I frame a task now instead of handing over a checklist. Instead of, "Post this caption at nine, use this hashtag set, check with me before you reply to any comments," I say something closer to this.

Here's the goal: this post should feel like it came from someone who actually knows our clients, and I want to see if a more personal tone gets more replies this month. Here's the guardrail: nothing about specific client results goes out without me seeing it first. Everything else, the wording, the timing, how you respond to comments, is yours to decide. Tell me what you tried and what happened.

That's longer to say the first time, and shorter every time after, because I'm reinforcing one way of thinking instead of writing a new instruction set for every task. A step-by-step list has to be rewritten every time the situation shifts. A goal and a guardrail travel with the person into situations I never anticipated.

When they decide something I wouldn't have

This is where most delegation attempts quietly fail, mine included, more than once. Someone makes a decision inside their authority that I would not have made. My first instinct used to be to take the decision back, fix it myself, and quietly narrow what I gave that person next time. That instinct undoes everything the framework is supposed to build: take the decision back the moment it goes differently than I would have chosen, and I've just taught that person the authority I described was never real.

💡
Coach the decision, don't reverse it

Ask what they were weighing when they made the call. Walk through what you'd have considered too. If the guardrail actually needed to be tighter, fix the guardrail for next time, out loud, so it's clear that's what changed. What you don't do is quietly redo the work or route the next version of that task back through yourself. That's the moment a person either becomes someone who thinks for the business, or someone who waits to be told.

How this scales as the team grows

The framework holds as the team gets bigger, but the discipline it takes from me changes. With one person, I can hold the goal and guardrails in my head and repeat them in conversation. With more people, I write them down, because the version I say on a Tuesday and the version I say on a Friday drift apart if I'm not careful, and drifting guardrails are worse than none at all. My job shifts from delegating individual tasks to making sure the goals and guardrails I've set are worth handing to someone else, and trusting the authority I gave out without checking every use of it.

I also started noticing who was asking to have decisions made for them, and who was making the call and reporting back. That difference matters more than experience level when I'm deciding who's ready for more responsibility. It's not about who's been here longest, it's about who's already operating like help instead of like hands.

Key takeaways

✓
Define the goal as the outcome, not the steps, so someone can adjust the how without asking permission.
✓
Keep guardrails to what genuinely needs your sign-off: budget, brand, legal, anything hard to undo. Everything else is a preference, not a guardrail.
✓
Hand over real authority for everything inside the guardrails, and mean it when a decision comes back different than you'd have made it yourself.
✓
Coach a decision you disagree with instead of reversing it. Reversing it teaches people to stop deciding.
✓
As the team grows, write the goals and guardrails down so they don't drift between one conversation and the next.
