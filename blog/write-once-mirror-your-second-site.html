Write It Once, Mirror the Second Site (How I Stopped Rewriting Every Post Twice)

Direct Answer

I run two sites: this one, my personal brand, written as I, and my agency site, written as we. I used to write every post twice, copying and rewriting the same idea for the second site. Now one post is the origin, and a mirror rebuilds it for the other site in that site's voice, for that site's reader, with its own examples and its own call to action. The two versions link to each other. Keeping both alive stopped being a writing problem and became a maintenance problem I only had to solve once.

The second site was always the one that went quiet. Not deliberately. I would publish something here, fully intend to adapt it for the agency, and then a client week would happen and the adaptation never came. Six months later one site looked active and the other looked abandoned, which is worse than not having it.

If you are running a personal brand alongside the company you founded, or any two sites where only one is genuinely being fed, this is the fix. The problem is not that you cannot write. It is that you are being asked to write the same thing twice, and nobody sustains that.

Consistency is a maintenance problem

This took me longer to see than it should have. I kept treating the quiet site as a discipline failure, something I would fix by planning better or blocking more time. But the actual cost was never the thinking. I had already done the thinking. The cost was doing the same work a second time in a slightly different costume, which is the least motivating task in existence.

Once I named it as maintenance rather than creation, the fix became obvious. Automate the boring transformation, keep the human part where it belongs, and stop asking myself to summon fresh enthusiasm for an idea I already had last Tuesday.

A mirror is not a copy

This is the part that matters most, and it is where people get it wrong. If you publish the same text on both sites, you do not get two assets. You get a weaker version of both. Search engines now have two candidates for the same query and no clear reason to prefer either, and readers who find both learn that one of them is filler.

A mirror has to actually change things. On this site I write as I, to a peer, using a story from my own week, and I end by inviting you to follow along. On the agency site the same idea is written as we, to someone evaluating whether to hire us, using a client shaped example, ending with an invitation to start a conversation. Same topic. Genuinely different pages.

Then they link to each other. The personal version points to the agency version for readers who want the business angle, and the agency version points back here for the personal take. That cross link is not decoration. It tells both search engines and AI search tools that these two pages are related and that each is the authoritative version for its own angle.

The exact setup, step by step

Step 1: Decide which site owns the topic

Before anything is written, pick the origin. If the idea comes from something that happened to me, it starts here and mirrors outward. If it comes from client work or a pattern we see across accounts, it starts on the agency site and mirrors inward. Getting this right first is what stops the mirrored version from reading like a translation of something that was never meant for that audience.

Step 2: Write the voice rules down once

This is the step that makes the whole thing repeatable, and it is the one people skip. For each site, write out in plain language: the pronoun, who the reader is, what they are worried about, what kind of example is allowed, and what the call to action is. Mine fit on half a page each.

You are not writing this for a tool. You are writing it because until you can state the difference between your two sites in five lines, the mirrored posts will keep coming out sounding like the same person in a slightly different mood.

Step 3: Generate the mirrored draft

With the origin post and the target site's voice rules, produce the second version. This gets you a structurally complete draft in the right voice, aimed at the right reader, in far less time than rewriting from a blank page.

Treat it as a draft. It will be about eighty percent right, and the missing twenty percent is always the same: the examples are too generic and the call to action is limp.

Step 4: The human pass that fixes what the mirror cannot

Replace the examples with real ones from that audience's world. Rewrite the opening so it sounds like it was written for them rather than adapted for them. Fix the call to action so it asks for the thing that site actually wants. Then read the first paragraph out loud, which is the fastest way to catch a version that is technically correct and completely lifeless.

This takes me about ten minutes. Rewriting from scratch used to take an hour and usually did not happen at all.

Step 5: Cross link both ways before publishing

Add the link in each direction as part of publishing, not as a thing you will tidy up later, because later does not arrive. One line near the end of each post is enough: here is the personal take, or here is the business angle. Do it in the same session or it does not get done.

What this does not fix

Not everything should be mirrored. Some posts are genuinely personal and would be strange coming from a company. Some are commercial and would be strange coming from a person. If you force every post across, both sites drift toward a bland middle where neither sounds like anyone. Roughly half of mine make the trip.

And a mirrored draft still needs a human before it goes live. The failure mode is not that the second version is wrong, it is that it is fine. Fine is what people scroll past. The ten minute edit is what makes the difference between two real posts and one real post with an echo.

Where this connects
