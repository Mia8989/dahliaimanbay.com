Write to One Person, Not a Crowd

Direct Answer

My copy was flat for years until someone told me the problem: I was writing to a crowd. Now I picture one real person, a customer or a donor I've actually talked to, and I write to them. I use "you," not "everyone." The post gets warmer, sharper, and it actually lands. Broad messaging is forgettable messaging.

Someone told me the problem with my copy once, and it stuck with me because it was so simple I'd missed it completely: I was writing to a crowd. Every sentence was built to apply to everyone, which meant it landed with no one in particular. I wasn't writing badly. I was writing to nobody.

Broad messaging is forgettable messaging

Here's the thing about writing for "everyone." It feels safe. It feels efficient, one message, maximum reach. But the moment you try to make a sentence true for every possible reader, you have to sand off every specific detail, because specific details are exactly what exclude people. What's left is smooth, general, and instantly forgettable, because nobody reading it feels like it was written for them. It was written for the average of everyone, and no real person is the average of everyone.

The fix isn't more polish. It's narrowing the audience in your head to one person while you write, even though the piece will still go out to everyone. That's the part that surprised me: writing to one person doesn't shrink your reach, it sharpens the writing enough that more people feel spoken to, not fewer.

How I actually picture the one person

This isn't a persona worksheet. I don't invent a fictional "ideal customer" with a made-up name and a stock photo. I think of someone real, a past customer or donor I've genuinely talked to, and I picture them specifically before I type a word. What did they actually say when they explained their problem to me? What was the moment something clicked for them? What would make them roll their eyes and stop reading?

A fictional persona is still a crowd wearing one name. It's built from averages and assumptions, so it produces the same flat writing a "target audience" does, just with a name badge on it. A real person carries detail you can't fake: the exact phrase they used, the specific worry they had before they said yes, the thing that almost made them walk away. That detail is what makes the writing warm instead of generic.

So before I write, I ask myself: who is this actually for? Not "who is this brand's audience," but which specific person, sitting somewhere real, is going to read this next. Then I write the whole thing to them.

The mechanical shift: from "everyone" to "you"

Once I have that one person in mind, the actual writing change is almost boring. I stop writing "our audience," "everyone," "people like you," and "small business owners everywhere," and I write "you." That's it. That's the whole mechanism. But the effect compounds across a paragraph, because every sentence built around "you" has to be concrete enough to be true for one person, which automatically cuts the vague, safe language that was making everything forgettable.

Before and after

Before: "Our clients often struggle to keep up with their marketing while running the rest of their business."

After: "You're running the whole business. Marketing is the thing that keeps sliding to the bottom of the list, not because it doesn't matter, but because everything else is on fire first."

Before: "Everyone deserves access to quality care, regardless of their circumstances."

After: "You shouldn't have to choose between paying rent and getting the care you need. That's not a fair choice, and it's not one we're asking you to make."

Before: "Our team is excited to announce a new resource for our community."

After: "If you've been asking where to start, this is it. We built it because you kept asking."

Why the same line got warmer AND sharper

Warmth and precision usually feel like a tradeoff, but writing to one person produces both at once. Warmth comes from direct address, "you," not "people." Precision comes from the fact that you can't write something vague enough for everyone and specific enough for one real person at the same time. The specificity forces the sharpness. The direct address delivers the warmth.

I wasn't writing badly. I was writing to nobody.

When this doesn't apply

This hack is for anywhere connection matters more than uniform coverage: marketing emails, social captions, landing pages, donor asks, sales copy. It is not for formal compliance copy, legal disclosures, or terms of service. Those need to say the same precise thing to every single reader, regardless of who they are, and warmth actively works against that goal. If you're writing something a lawyer needs to sign off on, write to everyone on purpose. Save the one-person approach for everything else.

Where to practice it

Three places to try this today

✓
Email. Before you write the subject line, picture the one person opening it. Write the whole email as if you're answering the question they actually asked you last time, not a generic update to "everyone on the list."
✓
Social captions. Swap "our community" for "you" in the first line. If the caption still makes sense when you imagine one specific follower reading it on their phone, it's working.
✓
Web copy. Read your homepage headline out loud as if you're saying it to the one person, face to face. If it sounds like something you'd actually say to them, keep it. If it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.

Key takeaways

  • Broad messaging is forgettable messaging: writing for everyone forces out the specific detail that makes writing land.
  • Picture a real past customer or donor, not an invented persona. Real detail can't be faked.
  • Replace "everyone," "our audience," and "people like you" with "you." That single mechanical swap does most of the work.
  • Writing to one person makes copy warmer and sharper at the same time, because specificity and direct address reinforce each other.
  • Save this for marketing, not for compliance or legal copy, where uniform precision matters more than connection.
