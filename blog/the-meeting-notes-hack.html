The Meeting Notes Hack That Let Me Actually Show Up

Direct Answer

I run a meeting recorder on my calls. It transcribes the conversation, summarizes it, and pulls out the action items automatically. After the call I get a clean recap and a task list, and I was actually present the whole time instead of typing while someone talked to me.

I used to sit in calls half-listening because I was too busy typing notes. I hated it. Someone would be walking me through a real problem, something that mattered to their business or their week, and half my attention was on getting the wording right in a doc instead of on them. I'd nod at the wrong moment. I'd ask a question they'd already answered two minutes earlier. Nobody ever said anything about it, but I knew, and it bothered me.

The cost of split attention nobody names out loud

Typing notes during a call feels productive. It isn't free. Every second spent getting a sentence down correctly is a second not spent actually listening to what's underneath it, the hesitation before someone answers a hard question, the detail they mention once and never repeat, the moment where the real ask finally surfaces. You can capture the words or you can catch the meaning. Doing both at once, in real time, is harder than it looks, and most of us are worse at it than we think.

It also costs the other person something. People can tell when you're partially there. A client walking you through a problem, a donor explaining what matters to them, a teammate flagging something they're nervous about: they read your attention the same way you'd read theirs. Being the person who is fully present in a conversation is not a soft skill you either have or don't. It's often just a logistics problem, and logistics problems are exactly what I try to automate.

What to actually look for in a meeting recorder

Not every tool in this category does the same job. Some just transcribe, which means you still have to read a wall of text to find what mattered. The setup worth having does more than transcribe. Here's what I check for before I trust one with a recurring call.

The four things that make it worth using

✓
Auto-transcription. A full, accurate written record of the call, generated without you starting or stopping anything manually. This is the baseline, not the whole value.
✓
Auto-summary. A short recap of what was actually discussed and decided, so you don't have to reread a full transcript to remember what happened on a call from three days ago.
✓
Action-item extraction. The tool pulls out who committed to what, separate from the general summary, so tasks don't get buried in a paragraph you have to comb through later.
✓
Calendar integration. It joins recurring calls on its own because it's connected to your calendar, instead of you having to remember to add it to every single meeting invite.

Setting it up for recurring calls

The setup itself is not complicated, which is part of why I recommend it so often. You connect the recorder to your calendar once. From there, it recognizes your recurring meetings and joins them automatically, the same weekly check-in, the same client call, the same standing sync, without you doing anything extra each time. The work happens once, up front, and then it just runs in the background for every future instance of that meeting.

I keep it scoped to the calls where I know I'll want a record afterward: client calls, team check-ins, anything with action items attached. I don't run it on every single call I take. A tool that records everything just replaces one kind of clutter with another. The calls where I need to actually think in real time, and not chase a summary later, I leave it off.

💬
Say it out loud too

Most meeting recorders announce themselves when they join a call, but I still say at the start of every recorded call that it's being recorded. It takes five seconds and it means nobody is ever surprised later.

What the post-call workflow actually looks like

The call ends, and within a few minutes I have two things waiting: a plain-language recap of what was discussed, and a separate list of the action items that came out of it, each one tied to whoever committed to it. I skim both. The recap tells me I didn't misremember anything, and the task list tells me exactly what happens next, mine and everyone else's.

The task list doesn't stay in the meeting summary. It goes wherever I already track work, the same task manager or project board everything else lives in. That part matters more than people expect. A perfect summary sitting in an email you never reopen is not actually useful. The value only shows up once the action items land next to the rest of what you're accountable for, where you'll actually see them again.

Your best team member should be in the conversation, not transcribing it.

The etiquette side: always disclose you're recording

This is the part I take the most seriously, and it's not optional. Every recorded call gets a disclosure, spoken out loud, at the start. That holds for internal team calls and it holds even more for calls with clients or donors, where trust is the entire relationship. Recording laws differ by state and country, some require everyone's consent and some only require one party to know, but I don't rely on the legal minimum. I tell people every time, regardless of what the law technically requires, because it's their conversation too and they deserve to know exactly how it's being captured.

In practice this is easy. Most tools announce themselves by name when they join the call, and I still say it myself in the first thirty seconds: "just a heads up, this call is being recorded so I can send you a recap after." Nobody has ever pushed back. If anything, people relax a little, because they know they don't have to repeat themselves and I'm not going to lose track of what they said.

Key takeaways

  • Split attention has a real cost: you catch the words or the meaning, rarely both, when you're typing while someone talks.
  • A meeting recorder worth using does four things: transcribes, summarizes, extracts action items, and joins recurring calls through calendar integration.
  • Set it up once per recurring meeting, then let calendar integration handle the rest. Keep it off for calls where you need to think in real time, not record.
  • Send action items to wherever you already track work, not to a summary you'll never reopen.
  • Disclose every recording, out loud, every time, especially with clients and donors. That is never optional.

This is hack 4 in a series I never expected to keep going past thirty, because there's always one more piece of the day quietly worth automating. If you sit in as many calls as I do, in a role where the conversation itself is the job, this is one of the simplest changes that actually gives you something back: your own attention.
